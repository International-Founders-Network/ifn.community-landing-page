A founder at the IFN meetup table has good news and a problem. They found the person they want: based in Austin, excited about the product, ready to start in two weeks. The home team assumed they would pay this person the way they pay freelancers at home, with a monthly invoice and a bank transfer. Then someone across the table asks whether the hire is a contractor or an employee. The founder starts to answer, stops, and admits they had not thought of it as a question.

Some version of this comes up most months. This post is peer orientation on what founders say they sort out before a first US offer goes out: how the person is classified, how pay actually runs, how much the role overlaps with the team at home, and who manages the hire day to day. It is not a hiring playbook, and it does not replace employment counsel or a CPA.

## Disclaimer

**This is not legal, tax, immigration, or financial advice.** IFN shares what founders in the room tend to talk through. Worker classification, payroll obligations, and what your own status allows you to do all depend on your facts, and the rules change. Verify with licensed professionals and official sources before you act.

## Contractor or employee depends on the work

At home, many founders could choose how to engage someone and write the contract to match. The surprise here is that in the US, the label on the contract does not settle it. Whether someone is a contractor or an employee depends on how the work actually happens, and more than one set of rules looks at it.

The IRS has its own guidance for tax purposes: [Independent contractor (self-employed) or employee?](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/independent-contractor-self-employed-or-employee) The US Department of Labor has guidance for federal wage law on its [misclassification page](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/flsa/misclassification). That guidance is changing: the Labor Department announced a final rule on Nov 19, 2026, published at 91 FR 75646 on Nov 23, 2026, that takes effect on Jan 22, 2027. It replaces the 2024 contractor test with an economic reality test centered on two core factors, how much control there is over the work and the worker's opportunity for profit or loss. The IRS test and state tests do not change with it, and states can apply their own rules on top, so read the current official pages and check what applies on your start date. Founders in the room do not try to walk each other through the tests. They point to the official pages and then to counsel.

What peers do share is how it felt to get it wrong. Founders who started someone as a contractor because it was faster, and then set that person's hours, tools, and priorities like anyone else on the team, describe the cleanup later as slower and more stressful than setting it up properly at the start. Founders who asked the question before the offer usually say the answer changed how they wrote the role.

## If it is an employee, a few things have to exist first

Hiring an employee in the US means someone has to be the employer. For most founders in the room that means a US entity that can run payroll, withhold and file taxes, and keep employment records. If the company is still being set up, the [forming from abroad post](/blog/forming-a-us-company-from-abroad) covers what peers sequence first.

A few pieces surprise people because they work differently at home, or do not exist there at all. US employers must complete Form I-9 to verify each new employee's identity and eligibility to work, including U.S. citizens, and USCIS keeps the [official I-9 page](https://www.uscis.gov/i-9). Form I-9 does not apply to independent contractors. The IRS has a [hiring employees page](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/hiring-employees) that lays out the federal side of being an employer. Texas has its own employer side too, including unemployment tax registration through the [Texas Workforce Commission](https://www.twc.texas.gov/businesses), and new hire reporting to the [Texas Employer New Hire Reporting Operations Center](https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/child-support/employers/new-hire-reporting), which the Office of the Attorney General administers. Employers must report new hires and rehires within 20 calendar days of the date the employee starts earning wages. Founders read these pages to know which questions to bring to a CPA, not to run payroll from a blog post.

## Payroll provider or employer of record

This is the decision founders compare notes on most, and the room keeps it at the level of how each option works.

A payroll provider runs pay for your own US entity. Your company is the employer. The provider handles the mechanics of paying people, withholding, and many of the filings, but the obligations stay with your company, and the person works for you directly.

An employer-of-record service employs the person on your behalf. The service is the legal employer, and you direct the work under a contract with them. Some founders use one to hire before their own entity is ready to employ anyone, or to hire in a state where they have no setup yet.

The tradeoffs peers mention:

- **Control.** With your own payroll you choose the benefits, the policies, and the paperwork. With an employer of record, much of that comes from the service's standard package.
- **Cost over time.** Founders often describe an employer of record as simpler at the start and more expensive as headcount grows, but the comparison depends on your numbers. Get real quotes and put them in front of your CPA.
- **The later move.** If you start with an employer of record and later bring the person onto your own entity, that is a real change for them: a new employer on paper, new benefits, new paperwork. Peers say to tell the hire up front if that is the plan.
- **Who the person feels they work for.** It sounds small, but it comes up. A first hire whose pay stub shows another company's name sometimes needs more reassurance that they are part of the team.

Neither option is the default. The choice depends on your entity, your timeline, and how many people you expect to hire here, which is why peers take it to a CPA or counsel before they choose.

## Decide the overlap before you post the role

Time zones get underestimated in hiring. If most of the team is many hours ahead of Austin, a US hire and the home team may share a sliver of the working day, or none of it.

Founders who handled this well decided how many shared hours the role needs before writing the job post, and said it plainly in the interview. A role that needs daily live work with engineers at home is a different job from one that looks after US customers and checks in with the team twice a week. The first US hire also tends to become the bridge between the two sides, which can mean early calls or late messages. That can work fine. It goes badly when the person finds out after they start.

## Someone has to manage this person

The gap peers describe most often is simpler than any form: the first US hire has nobody nearby. Their manager is asleep for most of their working day, there is no one to ask a quick question, and they become a team of one in a city where the company has no office.

Founders say it helped to decide who this person reports to before the offer, and whether that manager can give them a few live hours each week at a time that works for both. They also decided who answers questions during the hire's day when the manager cannot. Several flew the first hire to the home office early on, or brought someone from home to Austin for the first weeks, so the new person met the team as people and not only as names in a chat. Others gave the hire reasons to be around other founders here, which is one reason first hires sometimes come to the meetup with the founder.

If the founder is the one in Austin and becomes the default manager, peers suggest being realistic about the time that leaves. A founder who is also selling, raising, and settling a family is not always present for a new person's first month. The common advice in the room is short: ask the classification question before you pick the candidate, and have payroll or the employer-of-record contract ready before the start date, because the paperwork takes longer than the offer.

## When we stop talking

The room is useful for patterns. Bring in licensed help when any of these come up:

- Whether a role is a contractor or an employee, and the offer letter or contract terms: employment counsel
- Payroll tax registration, withholding, and comparing your own payroll with an employer of record: a CPA, ideally one who works with cross-border companies
- Whether you, as the founder, can hire, manage, or work in the business while you are in the US: immigration counsel, because it depends on your status and your facts

If you are not sure where to start looking, the [legal counsel post](/blog/how-international-founders-find-us-legal-counsel) covers what peers ask first. These are separate roles, and one professional rarely covers all of them.
