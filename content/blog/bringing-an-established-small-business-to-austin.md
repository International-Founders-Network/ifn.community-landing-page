An owner sits down at the IFN meetup table with a business card from home. The business on it has been profitable for years, with steady customers, a team, and suppliers who know them by first name. In their first weeks in Austin, three people have asked for something their name used to cover at home. A landlord wanted references and a local credit history. A supplier wanted payment up front. A first customer asked where they could read reviews. The owner is a little stung. Across the table, someone who brought their own business over a few years ago nods and says that was their first month too.

Some version of this comes up most months, and more often now that owners of established small businesses come to the room alongside startup founders. This post is peer orientation for that owner: what tends not to carry over to Austin, and what peers check early. It is not a relocation plan, and it does not replace a CPA, an attorney, or the city's own guidance.

## Disclaimer

**This is not legal, tax, immigration, or financial advice.** IFN shares what owners in the room tend to talk through. Permits, licenses, sales tax, entity structure, and what your status allows you to do depend on your business, your location, and your facts. Verify with licensed professionals and official sources before you act.

## Reputation starts close to zero

Years in business, awards, and loyal customers at home still matter. They show you know how to run something. But a customer in Austin usually cannot check them, so they lean on what they can check: reviews from people nearby, a referral from someone they know, a business they have seen around town.

Owners describe this as the hardest change to accept, because it feels like starting over. What worked for them was mostly patience. Take smaller jobs at first and do them very well. Ask early customers for an honest review, and make it easy to leave one. Show up locally, including in rooms like the meetup, where people refer each other. Owners who have talked about it in the room agree on shortcuts: do not buy reviews and do not fake them.

Put your history at home in context rather than leading with it. A line about how long you have done this work helps. A page of awards a local customer has never heard of usually does less.

## Supplier terms reset

At home, long relationships often come with payment terms, credit, and some flexibility when things go wrong. In Austin, a new business is a new customer to its suppliers, whatever its history elsewhere. Owners describe starting on prepayment or short terms until they had a few months of orders and payments on record.

That changes the cash plan more than people expect. If you are used to paying suppliers after your own customers have paid you, and here you pay first, the gap has to be covered from somewhere. Owners say to plan for it before opening rather than discover it in the first busy month. The entity and the business bank account behind all of this come first, and the [forming from abroad post](/blog/forming-a-us-company-from-abroad) covers what peers sequence.

## How prices are shown

Owners in the room spend less time on what to charge than on how to show it. A few differences come up again and again:

- **Tax added on top.** In many countries the price on the shelf or the menu includes tax. In the US the shown price usually does not, and sales tax is added at the register or on the invoice. Texas has a state sales tax, and a business selling taxable goods or services generally needs a sales tax permit from the [Texas Comptroller](https://comptroller.texas.gov/taxes/permit/). The permit itself has no fee, though the Comptroller [may require a security bond](https://comptroller.texas.gov/taxes/sales/faq/permit.php). Whether that applies to you, and how, is a CPA question.
- **Tipping.** In service businesses such as food and personal services, tipping norms can be very different from home. Owners talk about how it shapes menu prices, what customers assume, and what staff expect.
- **Estimates and quotes.** In trades and services, local customers may expect a written estimate first, or a fixed price, in ways that differ from home. Owners say it is worth asking a few local customers what they expect before writing the first quote.
- **Units and formats.** Imperial units, US date formats, and dollar pricing everywhere. Small details, but they tell a customer whether you set up for them.

## Permits and licenses: check before the lease

This is the item owners most often wish they had checked earlier. What you need depends on what you do and where you do it. A business can need permits or registrations from the city, the county, and the state, and some occupations need a license held by a person, not only by the business.

The part that catches owners is the space. Whether a given address can be used for your kind of business, and what work the building needs before you open, can decide whether the plan works at that location. Owners who signed a lease first and checked second describe it as a costly lesson. The ones who did it the other way asked the city before they signed.

The official starting points owners mention:

- City of Austin, [Development Services](https://www.austintexas.gov/development-services), for building permits, codes, and construction work on a space
- City of Austin, [Small Business Division](https://www.austintexas.gov/economic-development/divisions/small-business-division), for business training, coaching, and local events
- Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation, [tdlr.texas.gov](https://www.tdlr.texas.gov/), for many state-licensed occupations
- Texas Secretary of State, [business filings](https://www.sos.state.tx.us/corp/index.shtml), for entity and registration filings

Owners do not try to work out their requirements from these pages alone. They use them to learn which questions to ask, then confirm with the city and, where needed, an attorney or a licensed professional in their field.

## What does carry over

Not everything needs rework. Owners in the room say the craft travels: knowing the product, running a tight operation, treating customers well, leading a team. Many say the business model itself survived the move largely intact. What changed was everything around it: how people find you, why they trust you, how you get paid, and what the city needs from you before you open.

If you are still settling in yourself, the [first 90 days post](/blog/first-90-days-in-austin-as-an-international-founder) covers the personal side that runs alongside the business.

## When we stop talking

The room is useful for patterns. Bring in licensed help when any of these come up:

- Permits, zoning, and building use for a specific address: the City of Austin directly, plus an attorney or licensed professional (such as an architect or contractor) where the project needs one
- Sales tax, entity structure, and how money moves between the business at home and the one here: a CPA, ideally one with cross-border experience
- Lease review before you sign: an attorney
- Whether you can own, run, or work in the business while you are in the US: immigration counsel, because it depends on your status

These are different roles, and one professional rarely covers all of them.
