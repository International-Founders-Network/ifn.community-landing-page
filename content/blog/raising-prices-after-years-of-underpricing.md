An owner sits down at the IFN meetup table and says they finally did it. They raised their prices. They had put it off for a long time, rewritten the email more times than they want to admit, and then sent it on a quiet afternoon. Most clients wrote back some version of "thanks for the heads up, that's fine." Two did not. Those two were among the first clients the owner ever had in Austin, the ones who stayed when the business was small and unsure of itself. Both said they would look elsewhere. The owner feels relieved and a little sick, both at once, and wants to know whether they got it wrong.

The room hears some version of this most months, and people have strong opinions about it. The math tends to be the easy part. The hard part is pressing send and then waiting to see who stays. This post is peer orientation on how underpricing tends to start for founders who moved here, what peers say happened after they raised, and how they handled the conversation. It does not tell you what to charge, and it does not replace an accountant, a CPA, or counsel.

## Disclaimer

**This is not legal, tax, or financial advice.** IFN shares what founders in the room tend to talk through. Contract terms, sales tax, and whether your pricing supports your business all depend on your facts. Verify with licensed professionals and official sources before you act.

## How the low price got there

Few founders in the room set a low price on purpose. It crept in, usually for reasons that made sense at the time.

**Home-market numbers.** Some owners carried over prices that worked at home, sometimes converted and sometimes not, and never went back to check them against what the same work costs to deliver here. Rent, insurance, software and their own time can cost more here than the old price assumed.

**Pricing to win the first US customers.** With no local references and no network, a low number felt like the only way to get a yes. That first yes mattered. But the number that won it became the reference point for every client after.

**A discount that became the price.** One client got a break during a slow stretch. They mentioned it to a friend. The friend asked for the same. A year later nobody remembers it was ever a discount.

**Feeling like a newcomer who has to earn it.** This one gets the most nods. Many founders who came from somewhere else describe a habit of being grateful for any client at all. Every yes feels like a favor, and charging a full rate feels like asking for something you have not earned yet. Peers say this habit outlasts the business reasons. The client list grows, the references pile up, and the price stays where it was because raising it still feels presumptuous.

If you are earlier than this and still deciding how to price before you land, the [validation post](/blog/how-we-validate-a-us-market-before-landing) covers how peers test demand before a number hardens.

## What happened after peers raised

Nobody in the room claims raising prices is free of risk. Some clients leave. What founders describe is more mixed than the fear they carried beforehand.

The clients who left were often the ones who were already the hardest to work with. They pushed scope, paid late, or asked for one more round of revisions every time. Owners say they did not fully see how much those clients cost them until they were gone. Not every departure fits that pattern, though. Sometimes a good, loyal client leaves because their own budget is tight, and that one stings for a while.

The work usually got lighter. Fewer clients at a fair rate left room to do better work for the ones who stayed, or to take on new ones without burning out. Several founders say they did not realize how tired they were until that pressure eased.

Some clients came back. A few tried a cheaper option, found it did not fit, and returned, sometimes a little sheepish about it. Others never came back, and the owner made peace with that.

New clients who never knew the old price mostly did not react at all. To them it was just the price.

## How peers went about it

The room does not pass around scripts, and this is not the place for one. What comes up again and again is a handful of habits.

Peers gave notice. They picked a date some weeks out instead of making the change effective right away, so clients had time to plan and budget. If you have written agreements, check what they say about notice and price changes before you pick a date. That is a question for counsel.

They told their regulars first, in person or on a call, before any email went out. Owners say this was the conversation they dreaded most, and also the one that went best. A long-time client who hears it from you directly tends to take it differently than one who finds it in an inbox.

They were plain about why: costs went up, the work grew, the old number no longer reflected what they deliver. A sentence or two was enough.

And they tried not to over-explain. This is where the room gets most animated. Founders who wrote long, apologetic messages full of justification say they got more pushback and more requests to negotiate. The apology signaled the price was open for discussion. A short, warm, clear message landed better.

The room is split on grace periods for long-time clients. Some owners kept a few regulars at the old rate for a while as thanks. Others say that just delayed the same conversation. Fuller wording examples live with membership.

If you sell a product rather than a service, the conversation shifts toward packaging and tiers, which the [go-to-market post](/blog/us-go-to-market-when-your-product-was-built-elsewhere) covers.

## The feelings that come with it

Guilt comes up first. Owners feel they are letting down the people who believed in them early. Peers in the room tend to push back gently: a business that cannot sustain itself does not serve those early clients well either.

Then the small-community fear. Many founders in the room built their first client base through their own community: the same cultural association, the same place of worship, friends of friends. When clients are also neighbors, raising prices can feel like a social risk as much as a business one, and the worry is that everyone will hear and think less of you. Founders who went through it mostly say fewer people heard than they feared, and some who did hear respected the decision.

Then the quiet voice that says you are not worth it yet. Peers say waiting does not make it go away. It got quieter for them only after the email went out and the business was still standing. If the stress of it all starts to feel heavier than a pricing decision should, talking it through with a doctor, therapist or counselor is a reasonable step, separate from anything the room can offer.

## When we stop talking

The room is useful for patterns and for hearing that others have been through it. Bring in licensed help when any of these come up:

- Contract terms, notice clauses, and whether you can change prices during an existing agreement: counsel
- Sales tax on what you sell, and how a price change shows up on invoices and filings: a CPA
- Whether the business model works at the new price, including margins and cash: an accountant or another financial professional

Peers can tell you how the conversation felt. They cannot tell you what your numbers should be.
