A founder at the IFN meetup table is explaining a hiring decision and mentions, almost in passing, that their partner is back home with the kids while they build here. The conversation moves on. Later, as people are packing up, someone asks a simple question: how is your partner doing? The founder starts to answer and then stops. They realize nobody has asked them that in months. They are not sure they have asked it themselves.

Some version of this comes up most months. This post is about the person whose name is not on the cap table but whose life is tied to it: the founder's spouse or partner. For international founders the setup is often split across two countries. One adult is in Austin and the other is still at home, or a partner moved here and set a career aside. This is peer orientation from the room. It is a different conversation from moving the whole family (the [first 90 days post](/blog/first-90-days-in-austin-as-an-international-founder) covers more of the arrival side).

## Disclaimer

**This is not relationship, legal, tax, immigration, or financial advice.** IFN shares what founders in the room tend to talk through. What your partner and children are allowed to do here, how money and property work between you, and what your relationship needs all depend on your facts. Verify with licensed professionals and official sources before you act.

## What the partner carries

Founders talk about runway as a company number. At home, it is often a household number. In many of the stories told in the room, the partner's salary is what keeps the rent paid while the founder draws little or nothing. Or the savings funding the first year were shared savings, and the partner agreed to spend them.

Then there is the work nobody invoices. School runs, sick days, the repair person, the bills. A partner who stayed home with children is often parenting alone for long stretches, on a calendar set by the founder's flights.

There is the explaining, too. Parents and in-laws ask when the company will make money, why the founder is still abroad, whether this was a good idea. The founder can answer from a distance. The partner is the one sitting at the family dinner.

Underneath it is the waiting. The founder gets feedback every day: a call went well, a customer left, an investor went quiet. The partner gets the summary, usually late, and has to decide how worried to be with less information than anyone.

The Balderton Founder Wellbeing Report 2024, a survey of 128 VC-backed founders and CEOs almost all at European-based companies, found that 57% of those who were married or in a relationship reported a negative impact on their relationship with their spouse or partner. When it comes up in the room, people mostly recognize it.

## Two versions of the same strain

**The partner who stayed home.** Time zones decide when you talk. The founder calls at the end of their day, which lands at the start of the partner's, or the reverse, and someone is always tired. Peers describe calls that slide into logistics: who paid what, which form the school needs back. Visits help. They also reset the clock on missing each other, and the goodbye at the airport does not get easier. The founder who is not there can miss how much solo parenting costs until a visit makes it visible.

**The partner who moved.** From outside, this version looks easier. The family is together. But the partner who moved has often paused a career, sometimes because of what their own status allows (a question for immigration counsel, not the room), sometimes because someone had to run the move. The founder lands in a city with a built-in reason to meet people: customers, investors, rooms like this one. The partner lands in the same city with none of that. Founders who have lived it say the loneliness on that side is easy to miss because it is quiet.

## What peers say they do

This is not a program. It is what founders describe when they compare notes.

**Tell them the real numbers.** Founders who share the actual runway, the actual burn, and the actual pipeline say the conversations get calmer, even when the numbers are bad. A partner left guessing usually guesses worse.

**Agree on how long, and what counts as enough.** Some couples set a check-in point after which they sit down and decide again together. It might be a date, a revenue level, or a funding outcome. Then the decision belongs to both of them. The room does not suggest where that line should sit. That stays between the two of you.

**Give them their own way in.** A partner who moved to Austin needs people who are not there because of the founder. Some founders bring their partner to a meetup once and then step back. Others point them toward groups built around their partner's own field or interests. The [first 50 connections post](/blog/building-your-first-50-connections-in-austin) is written for founders, but much of it works for a partner starting from zero.

**Say thank you, specifically.** A general thank-you lands softer than naming the thing: the week of school runs while you were at a conference, the call with your parents they handled for you. Founders who started doing this say it costs nothing and they wish they had started sooner.

Some founders also say that talking with a couples counselor or licensed therapist, together or alone, helped them say things the weekly call could not hold. The room can point in that direction. It cannot do that work for you.

## When we stop talking

The room can share how other founders handled the strain. Bring in licensed help when any of these come up:

- Your spouse's or children's immigration status, including whether a partner can work here: immigration counsel
- Money, savings, and property between partners, especially across two countries: a lawyer or a licensed financial professional
- The relationship itself: a licensed counselor or therapist, together or on your own

If you or your partner are struggling day to day, a doctor or counselor is the right first call, before any peer group.
