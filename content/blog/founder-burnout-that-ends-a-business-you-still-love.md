An owner sits near the end of the IFN meetup table and says, quietly, that they are thinking of closing. The business is fine. Customers like it, some of them come back every week, and the numbers are not the problem. The owner says they feel empty. Someone across the table asks how long they have felt this way. The answer comes after a pause, and it is longer than they wanted to say out loud.

Some version of this comes up most months. This post is peer orientation on how burnout builds after a move to the US, why it can hit so hard in family and immigrant small businesses, and how owners in the room separate "I want to close" from "I need to step back." It is not medical or mental-health advice. It does not replace a doctor, a therapist, counsel, or a CPA.

## Disclaimer

**This is not medical, mental-health, legal, tax, or financial advice.** IFN shares what founders and owners in the room tend to talk through. How you are doing and what to do about your business both depend on your own facts. Talk to licensed professionals before you act on anything here.

## The business is fine. The owner is not.

When an owner says they want to close, the room's first instinct is to ask about revenue. That is often the wrong question. The product still works and the customers still show up. What has run out is the person holding it all together.

That is harder to say than "sales dropped." People understand the story of a failing business. When a healthy business has an owner who cannot keep running it, the owner tends to see it as a personal failure. So they wait, and they keep opening on time. By the time they say it at a meetup, they have usually been carrying it for a long while.

The owner at the table is not unusual. The 2023 Startup Snapshot survey found 36% of founders reported burnout. A 2025 study of 308 entrepreneurs globally, described by the authors as a preliminary sample, by researchers at Lehigh University, the Nasdaq Entrepreneurial Center and TU Dortmund found 74% indicated that the demands of business left them little room for self-care. Those numbers cover founders in general. The room adds another layer on top.

## How it builds after landing

Founders who moved here describe running two jobs at once. One is the company. The other is building a life in a new country: housing, schools, a driver's license, finding a doctor, working out which grocery store carries what you used to cook with. Neither job has an off switch. The second one never shows up on a task list, so it does not feel like work until it has worn you down. The [first 90 days post](/blog/first-90-days-in-austin-as-an-international-founder) covers that early stretch.

There is usually no backup. At home, a sibling might have covered the shop, a former colleague might have taken a call, or parents lived nearby. Here, the owner is often the only person who knows how everything works, so a sick day becomes a closed day.

Family businesses carry their own weight. When the business is the household income, and the household is sometimes the staff, stepping back can feel like letting everyone down at once. Owners from immigrant families describe businesses that have never taken a day off because someone in the family always opened up. For them, a break means stepping out of the family story as well as the work.

Then there is the belief that you have to earn the move. Many people in the room left something behind to come here: a career, family, a version of their life that was working. Some feel the business has to justify all of that, so rest starts to feel like wasting the sacrifice. Peers say this belief kept them going long after it had stopped helping them.

## Closing and stepping back are different decisions

The owner at the table said "closing." As the conversation goes on, that word usually splits in two. Closing ends the business. Stepping back changes the owner's role in it. Owners who are burned out often reach for closing because it is the only option that feels like a full stop.

Peers who have faced this describe the options they weighed before deciding. Nobody in the room recommends one over another, because the right choice depends on the business, the family, and the person.

- **A pause.** Closing for a set period, or cutting hours, and telling customers plainly.
- **Handing off a part.** Giving one piece of the work, often the piece that drains you most, to an employee, a contractor, or a service.
- **A partner.** Bringing in someone to share the load and the decisions, along with all the conversations that requires.
- **Selling.** Letting someone else carry what you built, sometimes with you staying on through a transition.
- **Closing.** Ending it on purpose, in a way you can live with later.

Several owners say that seeing the options laid out helped on its own. The choice stopped being "keep going exactly like this or shut it down." Some still closed. Some who were sure they would close handed off one part and kept the rest. Either way, they decided with more information than they had on the night they first said it out loud.

The room does not walk through how to sell or wind down a business. Those steps involve contracts, leases, staff, and taxes, and they belong with counsel and a CPA.

## What peers wish they had done earlier

Two things come up again and again.

The first is telling someone the truth sooner. Owners say they spent months giving everyone the polite answer ("busy, but good"), including people who would have helped. When they finally told a spouse, a friend, or a table of other founders, the problem did not go away, but they stopped carrying it alone. For some, the meetup was the first place they said it, because the people there understood the work and had no stake in the answer. If you are still building that circle here, the [first 50 connections post](/blog/building-your-first-50-connections-in-austin) has peer notes.

The second is looking at their own health before the business numbers. Owners describe checking sales every day while putting off their own checkups for much longer. Looking back, they wish they had treated how they felt as seriously as a cash-flow warning. A doctor, therapist, or counselor is the right person to talk to about how you are actually doing. Peers can tell you they went through something similar. They cannot tell you what is going on with you.

## When we stop talking

The room is a good place to be honest and compare notes. Peer talk ends here:

- Your health and how you are actually doing: a doctor or a licensed mental-health professional. In the US, the [988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline](https://988lifeline.org) is there any time if things feel like a crisis; you can call or text 988. 988 serves the United States and its territories, not other countries. See also [SAMHSA's 988 page](https://www.samhsa.gov/find-help/988).
- Closing, pausing, or selling the business: counsel and a CPA, before you announce anything
- Contracts, leases, loans, and obligations to staff: counsel who works with small businesses, and a CPA for the tax side
